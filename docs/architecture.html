Ledger core: architecture and trade-offs

Companion to github.com/mark-per/ledger-core. Decisions and the risks they defer, arising from the implementation; the rules themselves are in the repository's DESIGN.md and are not repeated here.

The implementation is five domain areas and one orchestrator: money (the shared value type at the currency's scale), the journal (append-only entries with a booking date and a value date; every balance derived on demand, nothing stored), the holds book (a projection beside the journal: promises, never movements), fees and interest (rules that read closing balances and write entries), and the replay (owns the clock, decides nothing about money). One principle drives every decision below: a day's closing balance is a question that can be re-asked later and get a different answer, because entries arrive after the day they belong to.

1. Append-only at scale

Where state grows without bound

Five structures only ever grow: the journal (every entry, forever); the holds book (every hold ever placed, including declined and expired, kept so the report can show them); the interest accruals (one unrounded figure per account per day); the set of processed operation ids that makes delivery idempotent; and the reporter's last-reported closing per account per day. None is trimmed, by design: append-only was the brief's rule and the simplest way to honour it was to keep everything.

What breaks first at 100×

Not memory. A hundred times the brief's stream is a thousand entries; a single process holds millions. What breaks is the scan. closing(account, day) walks every entry of the account on each call, and a day close calls it once per value day it re-checks, then again for every accrual it recomputes. The close is therefore entries × days: double the history and each close costs four times as much. At 100× volume with a year of history, the fee sweep after a back-dated entry (which re-checks from that value day to today) is where an operator first notices. The journal's linear growth is a cost; its quadratic re-reading is the failure.

The cheapest structural change

A per-account running balance by value date, maintained on append and owned by the journal. An entry adds its amount to its value day's total and to every later day's; a closing balance becomes a lookup; a back-dated entry costs an update from its value day forward, which is the same span the fee sweep already walks. No rule changes and the journal remains the source of truth; the index is a cache the journal keeps consistent, exactly as it already keeps the per-account close marker. The second change, which bounds the hot path rather than speeding it up, is a period cut-off: once a period is closed and its interest paid, carry its final balance forward as one opening figure and stop re-reading its entries. They stay in the journal (append-only is untouched) but leave the working set. The failing test in the repository is the case this change makes explicit: a correction into a paid period becomes a dated adjustment with a reason, not a silent rescan.

2. Value-dated entries in production

Every entry in this ledger has two dates, and the brief's one back-dated debit is enough to show why that is not a detail: it moves a day that had already been reported, charges fees three days late, and changes what the customer is owed. In a UAE-licensed bank the same two dates touch four surfaces.

  • Customer disclosure. The Central Bank of the UAE's consumer protection regime requires statements to be accurate and fees and interest to be disclosed and justifiable. A back-dated entry changes a statement a customer has already received and can create a fee or an interest difference for a period they believed was closed. Each restatement is therefore a customer-facing event: a corrected statement, a notice for a late fee, a complaint to answer. The report's "restated" lines in this implementation are the seed of that notice.
  • Regulatory and financial reporting. Period-end positions reported to the regulator and used for interest income under IFRS 9 are value-dated. An entry valued into a reported period changes a figure that has already left the bank. Banks handle this with a closed-period discipline: anything arriving for a closed period is booked as a dated adjustment in the current period, with the original period referenced, and material restatements are disclosed. This ledger has no closed periods yet; every day is open to back-dating, and section 1 names the fix.
  • Financial-crime monitoring. Transaction monitoring and reporting under the UAE AML/CFT framework runs on when the bank learned of a transaction, not when it is deemed effective. The booking date is the monitoring date; the value date is a reportable attribute. A ledger that kept only value dates would hide the lag between the two, which is itself a signal.
  • Internal fraud. The ability to post an entry with an arbitrary past value date is the ability to move interest between customers and periods. Banks treat it as a privileged action: limited to a role, limited in how far back it may reach, and never done by one person alone.

The one control before going live: a back-valuation gate in the journal itself. An entry whose value date is older than a configured limit, or falls in a closed period, cannot be appended directly; it needs a second approver and a reason, it is booked as a dated adjustment referencing the period it corrects, and it emits an event that drives the customer notice and the regulatory restatement log. The gate is one check in Ledger.append plus a reason and approver on the entry, both already half present as the Reason type on reversals. Everything else in the four surfaces above is a consumer of that event. I would add this before the business-day calendar and before persistence, because it is the only one of the three that stops money from being moved wrongly rather than recording it well.

3. Authorization lifecycle

In this model an authorization is a hold in the holds book, created when the issuer says yes or no and never a journal entry. It ends by a matching settlement (an active hold, a settlement at or below its amount, residual released) or in one of the ways below. Each row states what the model does today and what production would mandate.

Two related events are not endings of an authorization but belong beside them: a chargeback reverses a settlement weeks later (a REVERSAL of the settlement entry, with the dispute as its reason), and a second identifier, the approval code the issuer generates on a yes, would in production be carried on the hold and required on the clearing as proof of approval. The brief's settlements quote only the network's reference, so the model has one identifier.

Day close is a command driven by the stream; single writer, no locksKeeps the core free of clocks and threads; testable in milliseconds.Concurrent appends to one account would corrupt sequence order; production needs one writer per account or a lock, and a business-date roll so the close reads a closed set.
No persistence, no recoveryThe brief forbids it.The journal is lost with the process. Production appends each operation to a durable log before the ledger sees it and recovers by replaying that log into a fresh ledger: the holds book and accruals are projections, and the stress tests already check that the same stream yields the same report. A bug fixed after the fact is corrected the same way, by replay, never by editing state.
Refusals as report notes, not stored eventsDeclines and rejections move no money, so they are not journal entries.No queryable history of what was refused and why. Production routes every refusal and flag (declined authorization, rejected reversal, unmatched settlement, late or duplicate delivery) to a dead-letter topic with its reason, so engineering can fix the cause and re-submit the operation as a new one; nothing is edited.
Operations after the window close normally; no second periodLets the after-capitalization case exist for the failing test.Accruals for days after the window are computed and never paid; a real ledger needs a period calendar and a capitalization per period.
Amounts are positive; the kind gives the directionA debit of 100 is 100 out; "debit of −100" is refused rather than read as 100 in.Message formats that encode direction in the sign (a refund as a negative debit) must be translated before they reach the ledger.
One currency per account, no conversionThe brief has one currency per account and no exchange rate.A BHD amount sent to the AED account is refused. No FX and no multi-currency accounts; both are products a bank sells.

The pattern across the table: what was cut is either a message type the brief does not contain or a production concern (persistence, concurrency, calendars, periods) that the brief excluded. The rules themselves are complete for the stream given, and each cut names the one change that would be made first.
